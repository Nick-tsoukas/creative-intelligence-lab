# Research 005 — The Architecture of Attention

**Research date:** 2026-11-11
**Status:** Institutional-source review and interpretive visual study. Not a lab experiment, museum visit, complete-book reading or live-interface audit.
**Question:** How can visual relationships prioritize attention without sacrificing meaning, agency or usability?

## Key distinction
**Salience** concerns what stands out. **Comprehension** concerns what the viewer understands. **Preference** concerns liking. **Task success** concerns completing the intended action. These cannot be collapsed into one "eye-catching" measure.

## 1. Caravaggio — The Calling of Saint Matthew (1599–1600)
**Primary institutional record:** The Metropolitan Museum of Art, "Caravaggio (Michelangelo Merisi) (1571–1610) and His Followers": https://www.metmuseum.org/essays/caravaggio-michelangelo-merisi-1571-1610-and-his-followers

**Institutional claim:** The paintings for the Contarelli Chapel were unveiled in 1600. The Met identifies dramatic light, proximity to the picture plane and contemporary dress as key contributors to immediate narrative impact.

**Close-looking interpretation:** The light is not meaningful in isolation. A beam cuts across figures, pointing gestures establish human relationships, and expression helps determine the significance of the encounter. Strong contrast and narrative context cooperate.

**Design transfer hypothesis:** Create one dominant visual event supported by contextual cues. Do not let large bright surfaces compete with the action or content they exist to explain.

**Counterexample:** A dark, cinematic form with low-contrast field labels may attract attention to the hero but obstruct completion.

## 2. Johannes Vermeer — The Milkmaid (c. 1657–1658)
**Original source:** Rijksmuseum conservation research (September 8, 2022): https://www.rijksmuseum.nl/en/press/press-releases/rijksmuseum-reveals-major-discoveries-on-vermeer-s-painting-the-milkmaid
**Exhibition analysis:** https://www.rijksmuseum.nl/en/whats-on/exhibitions/past/vermeer/vermeer-exhibition-text

**Documented finding:** Technical examination found a jug holder and a fire basket that Vermeer later painted over. The Rijksmuseum describes the present composition's quiet atmosphere and the near-singular movement of pouring milk. This is stronger evidence of deliberate visual editing than a generic claim that "great artists use whitespace."

**Interpretation:** Removing two potential narrative competitors intensifies attention to one everyday gesture. The composition's economy does not mean lack of detail—it is selection.

**Design transfer hypothesis:** Eliminate unnecessary visual competitors around the central user action; preserve meaningful orientation and required controls.

**Counterexample:** Removing visible navigation labels to create a cleaner screen may harm discoverability and accessibility.

## 3. Saul Bass — Anatomy of a Murder (1959)
**Museum record:** https://www.moma.org/collection/works/4824
**Artist collection:** https://www.sfmoma.org/artist/Saul_Bass/

**Documented:** MoMA catalogues Bass's 1959 poster as an offset lithograph for Otto Preminger's film.

**Close-looking interpretation:** The disjointed body silhouette functions as a compressed visual concept. It is recognizable at a glance and sustains ambiguity appropriate to a crime film. The visual symbol and the typography have different jobs.

**Design transfer hypothesis:** Create a subject-specific visual metaphor that is meaningful before introducing decorative style.

**Counterexample:** Ambiguous pictograms are poor substitutes for labeled buttons in high-stakes workflows. A poster's productive mystery may become an application's usability failure.

## 4. James Turrell — Ganzfeld and Light and Space
**Institutional source:** Guggenheim's James Turrell exhibition materials: https://web.guggenheim.org/exhibitions/turrell/

**Institutional claim:** Turrell's installations investigate light as a perceptual material, including the Ganzfeld effect in unmodulated fields of color. His art can make observers aware of the act of seeing.

**Interpretation:** Some artistic experiences direct attention toward perception itself rather than a conventional focal object. Immersion can emerge from reduced visual boundaries and sustained color fields.

**Design transfer hypothesis:** When the subject warrants contemplation, atmosphere and sensory consistency can support meaning. But they cannot replace legible task structure.

**Limitations:** We have not experienced an installation in person. Museum documentation is not evidence of how every visitor perceives it. Avoid speculative neuroscientific claims.

## 5. Web attention — the F-shaped scanning myth
**NN/g primary analysis:** https://www.nngroup.com/articles/f-shaped-pattern-reading-web-content/
**Original 2006 study:** https://www.nngroup.com/articles/f-shaped-pattern-reading-web-content-discovered/

**Research-grounded claim:** F-shaped reading is one documented scan pattern, not a universal path. NN/g's 2017 analysis, last reviewed August 19, 2026, identifies other patterns and stresses how text formatting and meaningful cues affect reading.

**Interpretation:** Designers should not draw a giant F and assume users follow it. Scan behavior depends on task, text, interface structure, language and visual cues.

**Design transfer:** Put useful information in recognizable headings, front-load meaningful words, support scanning, and confirm comprehension with tasks rather than assuming a particular eye movement.

## Six mechanisms worth testing
1. **Luminance contrast:** attract notice; verify text contrast and attention costs elsewhere.
2. **Directional cues:** lines, gaze, gestures and alignment; ask whether they point to meaning.
3. **Selective removal:** lower competing signals without hiding controls.
4. **Symbolic compression:** combine complex narratives into memorable forms without making essential UI ambiguous.
5. **Atmospheric consistency:** let light, color and material support emotional experience while preserving control.
6. **Task-relevant information scent:** labels and grouping should tell users where to go next.

## Original test proposal
Prepare three versions of the *same invented content-discovery task*: (A) conventional hierarchical layout; (B) dramatic contrast with obvious labels; (C) sparse artistic composition. Keep content, controls and functionality identical. Recruit independent participants; record comprehension, first-click, completion time, errors, recall and preference. Test keyboard and small screens. The design with the strongest visual first impression may not win on task completion.

## Source / evidence integrity
Sources above are official museum or research/practitioner publications. Observations and transfers are explicitly our interpretations and hypotheses, not documented artist intentions. No performance or user testing occurred as part of this study. Artwork images should be linked to institutional hosts rather than committed without checking rights.
