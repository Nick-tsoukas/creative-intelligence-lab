# MA-003 — Light as boundary: Edward Hopper, *Nighthawks*

- **Creator / date / medium:** Edward Hopper, 1942, oil on canvas.
- **Primary museum record:** Art Institute of Chicago, Friends of American Art Collection 1942.51: https://archive.artic.edu/hopper/artwork/111628 ; dimensions 84.1 × 152.4 cm.
- **Image evidence:** low-resolution editorial photograph/crop of the actual painting viewed 2026-11-15; museum's source text read. **Full artwork image file, edges and painted surface not systematically inspected**.
- **Rights:** museum object copyright/image permissions must be reviewed before republishing. No illustration copied into project.

## Observed preview (limited)
A bright diner interior cuts a long horizontal illuminated strip across an otherwise dark, cool-toned street. At center-right, a few seated figures gather around a warm counter; the left glass facade curves toward the viewer. Exterior ground is visually empty and comparatively dim. Glass is an implied boundary between our vantage and the pictured people.

## Institution says
The Art Institute's discussion explicitly highlights newly introduced fluorescent illumination and the way the diner is staged as a luminous beacon. It notes Hopper eliminated any clear entrance, creating an unsettling sense of being kept outside despite the interior's inviting light. The institution includes Hopper's account of his original street reference and his qualification about implied loneliness.

## Interpretation / alternative
**Hypothesis:** a luminous interior can present a desirable human scene while a darker frame establishes atmosphere and a threshold. **Alternative:** for an actual interactive product, the absent entrance that enhances artistic exclusion is precisely what would create broken discoverability.

## Transferable mechanism
**Use when:** storytelling wants visitors to glimpse a real human moment in an atmospheric setting while making the route into the actual product unmistakable.

**Don't use when:** modeling a signup page, modal or mobile navigation; copying Hopper's missing doorway into product affordances is anti-UX.
