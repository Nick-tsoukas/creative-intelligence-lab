## Main passages and reasoning
### Chapter IV — Habit
James situates habits in repeated acts and physical plasticity. He draws from nineteenth-century neurological and physiological explanations and discusses how familiar repeated action can become easier and demand less deliberate attention. He gives a personal example of skill improvement after rest while learning a tune. The book's contents also explicitly distinguish “Produces ease of action”, “Diminishes attention” and chained performances as separate subsections. **We have inspected portions around the first of these mechanisms**, not all examples or scientific references.

**Interpretive UX transfer:** repeated, stable affordances can reduce the need for users to re-learn core operations. A Night Gallery send action that changes position, label and interaction model every time may prevent formation of an efficient familiar routine. However, **habit can entrench a harmful workflow**, so consistency does not validate a bad recipient model.

### Chapter XI — Attention
James begins by arguing that experience is not the passive registration of every stimulus. Selective interest makes certain things matter and others recede. Around printed pp. 402–404 he contrasts focused attention with distraction and argues that attention implies selecting one of several possible objects or trains of thought. He illustrates differing salience for people/animals perceiving the same setting. This is a useful historical philosophical statement of a problem, not modern evidence of an eye-movement prediction rule.

**Interpretive UX transfer:** the artist who enters a Night is likely attending to **the photos and people from that show**. The app should not assume the user's intention is to navigate a technical taxonomy such as “media object → campaign attachment → eligible segment.” This is an inference to verify with artists.

## Important counterexamples
- An experienced power-user may benefit from a shortcut that confuses a beginner; progressive disclosure/optional fast paths can reconcile this.
- A large bright banner may be ignored by someone searching for an expected label; interest and existing goals mediate attention.
- Modern cognition research can challenge James's nineteenth-century neuron/plasticity conjectures; do not teach his physiological speculations as fact.
- Habits are not uniformly desirable: automatic sending without an explicit recipient/visibility check is dangerous.

## Faneqo task hypothesis
Given a Night page, prioritize the user's meaningful action, explain the current audience and state, and permit repeat use of a stable flow. Ask the user to verify the recipient summary before broadcast to prevent the wrong habit from becoming an accidental send. Add recovery and interrupted-draft behavior rather than only a polished send animation.

## Skill translation
**Elite UX Director:** ask “What habit, prior expectation or learned term is this screen relying on?” Distinguish novice from expert needs and check irreversible recipient changes. **Creative Design Director:** attention hierarchy must follow the task's meaning and context, not simply decorate a hero photo; articulate the intended first, second, third attentional stops.
